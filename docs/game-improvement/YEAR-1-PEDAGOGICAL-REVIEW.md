# Year 1 Games — Pedagogical Review

**Reviewer perspective:** Secondary-school chemistry teacher, 20 years' experience, specializing in educational games.

**Curriculum reference:** Brown et al., *Chemistry: The Central Science*, Chapters 1–4.

---

## Executive Summary

The 10 year-1 games form a solid suite that covers most of the major topics in Brown chapters 1–4. The strongest games (Takmarkandi, Molmassi, Nafnakerfið) demonstrate genuinely excellent pedagogical design: scaffolded difficulty, tiered hints that address real misconceptions, and visual-first approaches before calculation. Other games (Lausnir, Gerðir efnahvarfa) are functional but would benefit from deeper feedback systems and misconception-targeting.

Several important chapter 1–4 topics are either absent or under-represented. Below I give game-by-game feedback, then describe suggestions for new games and cross-game improvements.

---

## Part 1: Game-by-Game Review

### 1. Flokkun efna (Classification of Matter) — Chapter 1.2

**What works well:**
- The visual classification tree (Matter → Pure Substances vs. Mixtures → Elements/Compounds vs. Homogeneous/Heterogeneous) mirrors Brown Figure 1.9 exactly. Students can build a mental model before being quizzed.
- Everyday examples (pizza, granite, bronze, air) are well chosen and relatable.
- Carbon-as-diamond and O₂ as element examples are thoughtfully selected.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add separation methods as a level.** Students at this stage need to understand *how* we classify matter, not just *what* the categories are. A level where students choose the correct separation technique (filtration, distillation, chromatography, evaporation, magnetism) for a given mixture would reinforce *why* the homo/heterogeneous distinction matters. This directly supports Brown Section 1.3.

2. **Address the "is air an element?" misconception.** Many first-year students believe air is a single substance. The game includes air as a homogeneous mixture, but the feedback should explicitly say *why* — it contains N₂, O₂, Ar, CO₂ in fixed proportions but is not chemically bonded. A brief composition breakdown when the student encounters this item would be valuable.

3. **Add physical vs. chemical change examples with video/animation.** The current Level 3 covers physical vs. chemical properties/changes, but this is one of the hardest distinctions for students. Showing a short animation of ice melting (physical) versus iron rusting (chemical) with the question "what evidence tells you this is a chemical change?" would be more effective than text-only descriptions. Key evidence: gas production, color change, energy release, precipitate formation.

4. **Include the "pure" terminology trap.** Students confuse "pure" in everyday language (pure water = clean) with "pure" in chemistry (pure substance = single element or compound). This misconception deserves an explicit question: "Is filtered tap water a pure substance?" (No — it still contains dissolved minerals.)

### 2. Markverðir tölustafir (Significant Figures) — Chapter 1.5

**What works well:**
- Three-level progression (identify → calculate → scientific notation) is the right sequence.
- Rules for leading zeros, captive zeros, and trailing zeros are covered.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add measurement-reading problems.** Significant figures exist because of measurement uncertainty, but the game treats them as abstract number rules. Adding a level where students read a graduated cylinder, ruler, or balance image and record the measurement to the correct number of sig figs would ground the concept in laboratory reality. This is one of the most common exam question types.

2. **Explicitly teach the "exact numbers" exception.** Counting numbers (12 eggs), defined conversions (1 km = 1000 m), and coefficients in balanced equations have infinite sig figs. Students routinely get tripped up by using the "2" in "2H₂O" as a sig fig limit. This deserves at least 2–3 questions.

3. **Add mixed-operation problems.** Real chemistry calculations involve both multiplication and addition in the same problem (e.g., calculating molar mass then using it). Students need practice deciding which rule applies at each step. Currently the game separates these, but a combined challenge would be more authentic.

4. **Consider adding estimation/order-of-magnitude questions.** Brown Section 1.5 emphasizes that sig figs reflect precision. A question like "Estimate whether 3.2 × 10⁴ + 5.1 × 10² is closer to 3.2 × 10⁴ or 3.7 × 10⁴" would build number sense alongside rule-following.

### 3. Dimensional Analysis (Einingagreining) — Chapter 1.6

**What works well:**
- The visual-first approach (Level 1: no calculations, just concepts) is excellent pedagogy.
- Level 4 bridges into chemistry-specific conversions (grams ↔ moles ↔ particles), which directly supports Molmassi.
- Inclusion of gas volume at STP (22.4 L/mol) is appropriate for this level.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add a "conversion factor construction" level.** The hardest part of dimensional analysis for students is not the arithmetic — it is knowing which conversion factor to write and which way to orient it. A drag-and-drop level where students build a conversion pathway (e.g., arrange "1 mol / 6.02×10²³ molecules" vs. "6.02×10²³ molecules / 1 mol" in the correct orientation) would target the actual difficulty.

2. **Include multi-step unit conversion chains.** Brown emphasizes that dimensional analysis is powerful because conversions chain together. Problems like "Convert 25.0 g of Fe to number of atoms" require: grams → moles (÷ molar mass) → atoms (× Avogadro). Students should see the entire chain laid out visually before computing.

3. **Add density as a conversion factor.** Brown Section 1.4 introduces density (d = m/V) as a conversion factor between mass and volume. This is often the first place students encounter dimensional analysis, and it is missing. Problems like "A block of aluminum has a volume of 15.0 cm³. Given density = 2.70 g/cm³, what is its mass?" are standard exam questions.

4. **Include unit conversion with temperature.** Celsius ↔ Kelvin is a non-proportional conversion (K = °C + 273.15) that breaks the "multiply by a conversion factor" pattern. Students need to see this exception early. Even 2–3 temperature conversion questions would address this.

### 4. Nafnakerfið (Nomenclature) — Chapter 2.8

**What works well:**
- 59 compounds across three difficulty tiers is a substantial database.
- Covering ionic, molecular, and acid nomenclature in one game mirrors Brown's chapter 2.8 structure.
- The memory-matching game (Level 3) is an effective recall strategy.
- The "name builder" bonus mode (constructing names from prefixes/suffixes) targets procedural knowledge.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add polyatomic ion flash-card drill.** Students consistently report that memorizing polyatomic ions is their biggest nomenclature challenge. A dedicated mini-game (rapid-fire matching of formula ↔ name for SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, PO₄³⁻, CO₃²⁻, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, ClO₃⁻, etc.) with spaced repetition would be more effective than encountering them only within compounds.

2. **Strengthen the Stock system (Roman numerals) section.** Variable-charge metals (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Mn²⁺/Mn⁴⁺/Mn⁷⁺) are a major source of exam errors. The game includes some of these but should have a dedicated level where students must determine the charge from the formula (e.g., "What is the charge on iron in Fe₂O₃?" → +3) before naming.

3. **Add a "formula from name" direction.** The game emphasizes name-from-formula, but students also need to write formulas from names. "Write the formula for calcium phosphate" → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ requires understanding charge balancing, which is a distinct skill from reading a formula.

4. **Include common/trivial names as a bonus.** Water, ammonia, methane — students must recognize that these are trivial names with no systematic etymology. A quick matching exercise would prevent confusion.

5. **Add binary molecular compound prefixes more explicitly.** The Greek prefix system (mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-) for molecular compounds (e.g., dinitrogen pentoxide = N₂O₅) deserves its own focused exercise. Students commonly forget prefixes or confuse them with ionic naming.

### 5. Stilltu efnajöfnur (Balancing Equations) — Chapter 3.1

**What works well:**
- Visual atom-by-atom balancing (Level 1) is the gold standard approach for teaching this skill.
- Progression from guided (Level 2) to unassisted (Level 3) is appropriate.
- The equation database includes all major reaction types.
- Including the genuinely difficult Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + SiO₂ + C equation in the hard tier is good — students need to see that balancing can be complex.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add a "balancing by inspection" strategy tutorial.** Most students try random coefficients. Teaching the systematic approach (balance metals first, then nonmetals, then hydrogen, then oxygen last) as a guided walkthrough before the practice levels would dramatically improve success rates.

2. **Show atom inventory tables.** For each equation attempt, showing a live table of "atoms on left | atoms on right" for each element gives students immediate feedback on what is unbalanced. This is more effective than just "incorrect — try again."

3. **Include fractional coefficient → whole number conversion.** Some equations are easier to balance using ½ first (e.g., C₂H₆ + 7/2 O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O, then multiply through by 2). Showing this technique builds mathematical flexibility.

4. **Add conservation of mass verification.** After balancing, have students calculate the total mass on each side to verify. This reinforces *why* we balance equations (law of conservation of mass) rather than treating it as a purely symbolic exercise.

### 6. Molmassi (Molar Mass & Avogadro's Number) — Chapter 3.3–3.4

**What works well:**
- Five levels is the deepest progression of any game. The arc from atom-counting → mass estimation → precise calculation → Avogadro → conversion chains mirrors exactly how Brown builds the concept.
- The mystery-molecule bonus is creative and motivating.
- 24+ elements with accurate atomic masses.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add isotope-aware atomic mass problems.** Brown 3.3 introduces average atomic mass from isotope abundances. At least a few problems where students calculate average atomic mass (e.g., chlorine: 75.77% ³⁵Cl + 24.23% ³⁷Cl = 35.45 amu) would connect to the periodic table values they use elsewhere.

2. **Include hydrate molar masses.** Compounds like CuSO₄·5H₂O appear in first-year labs. Calculating their molar mass (249.7 g/mol) requires understanding that the water of crystallization is part of the formula. This is a common exam problem.

3. **Add a "which has more molecules?" comparison level.** Problems like "Which contains more molecules: 10 g of H₂O or 10 g of CO₂?" force students to convert to moles and compare, which tests conceptual understanding rather than just calculation.

4. **Strengthen the conversion chain visualization.** The Level 5 conversion chains are conceptually demanding. Adding a visual "road map" that students can refer to (grams ↔ moles ↔ molecules ↔ atoms, with the conversion factor on each arrow) would help students see the big picture.

---

### 7. Hlutfallsgreining (Percent Composition & Empirical Formulas) — Chapter 3.5

**What works well:**
- Three-level progression (mass % → empirical formula → molecular formula) matches Brown exactly.
- The glucose example (C₆H₁₂O₆ from 40% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O) is the classic textbook problem.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add combustion analysis problems.** Brown Section 3.5 introduces empirical formula determination from combustion data (mass of CO₂ and H₂O produced). This is a distinct problem type from "given percentages." A level where students work backward from combustion products to empirical formula would round out the coverage.

2. **Show the step-by-step method more explicitly.** The conversion chain (% → assume 100 g → grams → moles → divide by smallest → round to integers) has 5 steps. Guiding students through each step with validation at each point (not just checking the final answer) would catch errors earlier.

3. **Add problems with non-obvious rounding.** Some empirical formulas require multiplying through by 2 or 3 (e.g., ratios of 1:1.5 → multiply by 2 to get 2:3). Students frequently round 1.5 to 2 instead. Including several of these "trick" ratios with explicit feedback would address this common error.

4. **Include real-world analytical chemistry context.** Frame some problems as "a forensic lab analyzed an unknown white powder" or "a pharmaceutical company needs to verify a drug's composition." This provides motivation and shows why empirical formula determination matters.

### 8. Takmarkandi (Limiting Reactants & Percent Yield) — Chapter 3.7

**What works well:**
- This is the strongest game pedagogically. The tiered hint system (topic → strategy → method → solution) with point penalties is best-in-class.
- Explicit misconception feedback ("Limiting reagent isn't always the smaller amount") directly targets the #1 student error.
- Dual-mode Level 2 (molecular count vs. grams/moles) lets students build from concrete to abstract.
- Factory mode as a bonus is creative and makes stoichiometry feel consequential.
- 20 reactions across three difficulty tiers, all correctly balanced, with accurate molar masses.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add a visual "reaction progress" animation.** Show molecules reacting pair-by-pair until the limiting reactant runs out, leaving excess reactant molecules behind. This visual would make the abstract concept visceral. Many students can calculate the answer but don't truly understand *what is happening* at the molecular level.

2. **Include problems where the limiting reactant switches.** Give students two scenarios with the same reaction but different amounts, where reactant A limits in one case and reactant B limits in the other. This prevents the pattern-matching shortcut of "the one listed first usually limits."

3. **Add percent yield with explicit error sources.** Level 4 teaches percent yield calculation, but doesn't explain *why* yields are below 100%. Adding context (side reactions, incomplete reactions, loss during transfer, impure reagents) would connect the math to real laboratory experience.

4. **Factory mode could teach optimization.** Currently it is somewhat disconnected from stoichiometry learning. If factory mode required students to calculate optimal reactant purchases to minimize waste (i.e., buy reagents in stoichiometric ratios), it would reinforce the limiting reactant concept rather than being a separate game.

### 9. Lausnir (Solutions & Molarity) — Chapter 4.5

**What works well:**
- Visual solute/solvent manipulation in Level 1 grounds the concept before formulas.
- Real solubility data for KNO₃, NaCl, sugar, and CaSO₄ with interpolation.
- CaSO₄ retrograde solubility is a sophisticated inclusion that challenges the "heating always dissolves more" misconception.
- The M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ dilution problems are essential exam preparation.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add a solution preparation simulation.** "Prepare 250 mL of 0.100 M NaCl" — student must calculate grams needed, then "weigh" and "dissolve" in a virtual lab. This procedural exercise mirrors actual lab work and is a common exam question type.

2. **Add electrolyte vs. non-electrolyte classification.** Brown Section 4.1 distinguishes strong electrolytes (NaCl, HCl), weak electrolytes (CH₃COOH), and non-electrolytes (glucose, ethanol). A sorting exercise would directly support the chapter 4 content. This could include a visual of a conductivity tester lighting a bulb (bright = strong, dim = weak, off = non).

3. **Explain retrograde solubility explicitly.** CaSO₄ is included in the problem set but students may not understand *why* it behaves differently. A brief explanation (some dissolution reactions are exothermic, so heating shifts equilibrium toward undissolved solid — a preview of Le Chatelier's principle) would turn a surprising fact into a learning moment.

4. **Add solution stoichiometry problems.** Brown Section 4.6 covers using molarity in stoichiometry calculations: "How many mL of 0.200 M NaOH are needed to neutralize 25.0 mL of 0.150 M HCl?" This bridges the solutions game to the stoichiometry games and is heavily tested.

5. **Include colligative property teasers.** Even a simple question like "Which freezes at a lower temperature: pure water or salt water?" plants a seed for year 2 topics and connects to everyday experience (road salt).

### 10. Gerðir efnahvarfa (Reaction Types) — Chapter 4.1–4.4

**What works well:**
- All five classic reaction types (synthesis, decomposition, single replacement, double replacement, combustion) with 20 correctly balanced examples.
- Real-world context for combustion reactions (methane/natural gas, propane/grills, octane/gasoline, glucose/respiration).
- Including respiration as combustion is a clever cross-disciplinary connection.

**Suggestions for improvement:**

1. **Add driving force analysis for double replacement.** Students memorize the pattern (AB + CD → AD + CB) but don't understand *why* some reactions occur. Brown Section 4.2 emphasizes that double replacement reactions proceed when they produce: a precipitate, a gas, or water (a weak electrolyte). Adding questions like "Will this reaction occur? What drives it?" would build understanding rather than pattern recognition.

2. **Include solubility rules for precipitation prediction.** This is a major chapter 4 topic. A sub-level where students use solubility rules to predict whether a precipitate forms (e.g., "Mix NaCl and AgNO₃ — is AgCl soluble?" → No, halides of Ag are insoluble) would directly support Brown Section 4.2. Students should be given a solubility table and asked to use it.

3. **Add activity series for single replacement prediction.** Brown Section 4.4 introduces the activity series. Questions like "Will zinc displace copper from CuSO₄ solution?" (Yes, Zn is above Cu) are standard and should be practiced alongside the reaction type classification.

4. **Separate neutralization from double replacement.** The game correctly classifies HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O as double replacement, but students need to understand that acid-base neutralization is a *specific type* of double replacement. This distinction matters because neutralization always produces water + a salt, and this pattern is heavily tested.

5. **Add net ionic equation writing.** This is one of the hardest chapter 4 skills. A level where students go from molecular equation → complete ionic equation → net ionic equation would be extremely valuable. Start with precipitation reactions (e.g., Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)) which are the most intuitive.

6. **Include oxidation-reduction identification.** Brown Section 4.4 introduces oxidation states and redox reactions. At minimum, students should be able to identify which element is oxidized and which is reduced in single replacement reactions. This doesn't require balancing redox equations — just tracking electron transfer.

### Proposed New Games

#### A. "Uppbygging atómanna" (Atomic Structure) — HIGH PRIORITY

**Why:** Atomic structure is foundational. Without understanding protons, neutrons, and electrons, students cannot reason about ions, isotopes, or the periodic table. Brown devotes an entire chapter section (2.3) to this.

**Concept:**
- **Level 1:** Build atoms. Given an element and its mass number, students place the correct number of protons, neutrons, and electrons into a Bohr model diagram. Visual, tactile, immediate feedback.
- **Level 2:** Isotope identification. Given atomic number and mass number, determine the number of each subatomic particle. Include isotope notation (e.g., ¹⁴₆C).
- **Level 3:** Average atomic mass calculation from isotope abundances. "Chlorine has two stable isotopes: ³⁵Cl (75.77%) and ³⁷Cl (24.23%). Calculate the average atomic mass." This is a standard exam problem.
- **Level 4 (Bonus):** Ion formation. Remove or add electrons to form cations/anions. "Na loses 1 electron → Na⁺ (10 electrons, 11 protons)." This bridges to nomenclature.

**Common misconceptions to target:**
- "Protons and electrons are always equal" (not in ions!)
- "Mass number = atomic mass on periodic table" (no — atomic mass is a weighted average of isotopes)
- "Neutrons don't matter" (they determine isotope identity and nuclear stability)

---

#### B. "Lotukerfið" (Periodic Table Explorer) — HIGH PRIORITY

**Why:** The periodic table is the organizing framework for all of chemistry. Brown Section 2.5 covers groups, periods, metals/nonmetals/metalloids, and periodic trends. Students need to be fluent in reading the table.

**Concept:**
- **Level 1:** Element lookup challenges. Given clues (atomic number, symbol, or name), locate elements on an interactive periodic table. Timed for engagement.
- **Level 2:** Classification. Sort elements into metals, nonmetals, and metalloids. Identify alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, halogens, noble gases, and transition metals by group.
- **Level 3:** Periodic trends (qualitative). "Which has a larger atomic radius: Na or Cl?" "Which has a higher ionization energy: Li or F?" Teach trends across periods and down groups.
- **Level 4 (Bonus):** Predict ion charges from position. Group 1 → +1, Group 2 → +2, Group 17 → -1. This directly feeds into nomenclature.

**Common misconceptions to target:**
- "Metals are always solid" (mercury is liquid)
- "The periodic table is just for looking up masses" (it predicts properties!)
- "Noble gases don't do anything" (they reveal electron configuration patterns)

---

#### C. "Jónir í lausn" (Ions in Solution — Precipitation & Net Ionic Equations) — HIGH PRIORITY

**Why:** Brown Chapter 4 devotes three full sections (4.1–4.3) to reactions in aqueous solution. Precipitation reactions with solubility rules are among the most heavily tested topics in first-year chemistry, and they are currently absent from the game suite.

**Concept:**
- **Level 1:** Strong vs. weak vs. non-electrolyte sorting. Classify common substances (NaCl → strong, CH₃COOH → weak, C₆H₁₂O₆ → non). Include conductivity-tester visual.
- **Level 2:** Solubility rules application. Given a solubility rules table, predict whether compounds are soluble or insoluble. "Is PbCl₂ soluble?" (No — Cl⁻ is usually soluble, but Pb²⁺ is an exception.) Start with simple cases, build to exceptions.
- **Level 3:** Predict precipitation. "Mix solutions of Pb(NO₃)₂ and KI. Will a precipitate form? What is it?" → PbI₂ (yellow precipitate). Students apply solubility rules to real mixing scenarios.
- **Level 4:** Write net ionic equations. Strip out spectator ions to reveal the essential chemistry: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s). This is a critical chapter 4 skill.

**Common misconceptions to target:**
- "All ionic compounds dissolve in water" (no — solubility rules exist precisely because many don't)
- "Spectator ions react" (they don't — that's what makes them spectators)
- "Net ionic equations are just shorter versions" (they reveal the actual chemistry happening)

---

#### D. "Sýrur og basar" (Acids & Bases — Neutralization) — HIGH PRIORITY

**Why:** Brown Section 4.3 covers acid-base reactions, and this is inadequately represented. The gerðir-efnahvarfa game includes one neutralization example but doesn't teach the concept systematically.

**Concept:**
- **Level 1:** Identify acids and bases. Sort common substances (HCl, NaOH, H₂SO₄, Ca(OH)₂, HNO₃, KOH) and learn to recognize the patterns (acids: H + anion; bases: metal + OH).
- **Level 2:** Write neutralization equations. acid + base → salt + water. Students identify the salt produced: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.
- **Level 3:** Neutralization stoichiometry. "How many mL of 0.10 M NaOH are needed to neutralize 25 mL of 0.15 M HCl?" This integrates molarity from Lausnir with stoichiometry from Takmarkandi.
- **Level 4 (Bonus):** Identify strong vs. weak acids/bases. HCl (strong) vs. CH₃COOH (weak). Connect to electrolyte concept.

**Common misconceptions to target:**
- "All acids are dangerous" (vinegar is acetic acid; citric acid is in lemons)
- "Neutralization always produces pH 7" (only for strong acid + strong base)
- "Bases are the opposite of acids" (they are complementary, not opposite — both are corrosive in concentrated form)

## Part 3: Cross-Cutting Recommendations

### 1. Adaptive Difficulty

No game currently adjusts difficulty based on student performance. If a student misses 3 consecutive questions, the game should offer easier problems or additional scaffolding. If a student answers 5 in a row correctly, the game should advance to harder content. This is well-established in educational research (Vygotsky's zone of proximal development) and would significantly improve learning outcomes.

### 2. Spaced Repetition / Review Mode

Students forget. A "daily review" mode that pulls 5–10 questions from previously completed games (weighted toward items the student previously got wrong) would dramatically improve long-term retention. This is particularly important for nomenclature and polyatomic ions, which require memorization.

### 3. Cross-Game Integration Challenges

Currently each game is self-contained. A capstone challenge that requires combining skills from multiple games would test deeper understanding. Example: "Given 5.00 g of Na₂CO₃ and 100 mL of 0.200 M HCl, identify the reaction type, determine the limiting reactant, calculate the theoretical yield of CO₂, and express your answer to the correct number of significant figures." This single problem touches Gerðir efnahvarfa, Takmarkandi, Molmassi, Lausnir, and Markverðir tölustafir.

### 4. Laboratory Connection

Chemistry is an experimental science. Several games would benefit from a "lab mode" where problems are framed as lab procedures:
- Lausnir: "Prepare 250 mL of 0.100 M NaCl solution"
- Takmarkandi: "You have 2.50 g of Mg and 100 mL of 1.0 M HCl. Design an experiment."
- Hlutfallsgreining: "You burned 1.000 g of an unknown compound and collected 2.637 g CO₂ and 1.079 g H₂O. What is the empirical formula?"

This makes the math feel purposeful rather than abstract.

### 5. Error Analysis Mode

Add a mode where students are shown a *wrong* solution and must find the error. Example: "A student calculated the molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ as 57 g/mol. What did they do wrong?" (They forgot to multiply OH by 2; correct answer is 74.1 g/mol.) Error analysis develops deeper understanding than just solving problems correctly, and it mirrors how scientists actually think.

### 6. Misconception-Aware Feedback for All Games

Takmarkandi's tiered hint system with misconception-specific feedback is the best in the suite. This approach should be adopted by all games. When a student gives a specific wrong answer, the feedback should address *why that particular wrong answer is tempting*. For example, in Nafnakerfið, if a student names FeCl₃ as "iron chloride" (omitting the Roman numeral), the feedback should say "Iron can form Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ ions. You need the Roman numeral (III) to specify which one."

### 7. Icelandic Chemistry Terminology

The games do an excellent job of using Icelandic chemistry terminology (efnasamband, frumefni, mól, etc.). One addition: include a glossary popup accessible from any game that maps Icelandic terms ↔ English terms ↔ chemical symbols. Students working with Brown (an English textbook) alongside Icelandic-medium instruction may struggle with terminology mapping.

## Conclusion

The existing year 1 game suite is a strong foundation with accurate chemistry content and generally good pedagogical design. The main gaps are in Brown Chapter 2 (atomic structure, periodic table) and Chapter 4 (aqueous reactions, precipitation, acid-base chemistry). Filling these gaps with 3–4 new games and enhancing the feedback systems in existing games would create comprehensive coverage of the first-year curriculum. The Takmarkandi game's tiered hint system and misconception-aware feedback should serve as the model for all other games.
